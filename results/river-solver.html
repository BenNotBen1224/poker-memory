Record cards, players, actions, amounts, and times. This demo's hand was checked by a person.

Rebuild the pot, stacks, and legal choices at each action.

3

Assume possible hands

Build a range: possible two-card hands and their weights. Do not feed Joe's revealed cards into the solver.

4

Solve the river

Compare legal bets, calls, raises, and folds for both players within a limited set of bet sizes.

5

Explain the choices

Show estimated average chip results, assumptions, and the matching video moments.

The hand record is observed. Joe's range is assumed. Before the river, this model filters hands after calls only. Bets, raises, and checks do not narrow those starting ranges. It is not a learned profile of Joe.

The river solver then weights Joe's possible hands by how often its strategy takes the recorded line. That assumption drives the call estimate below.

GTO means game-theory optimal. This is an approximate solution to a restricted two-player river game, not a solution to the entire tournament.

3. EV means an average result, not a bet amount

Expected value, or EV, is the average net chips a choice would return across the model's possible opposing hands and responses. A positive number means an average gain. A negative number means an average loss.

On this river there are no cards left to come. The uncertainty is what Joe holds and how he responds, not which card arrives next.

EV = chance of winning × net gain when winning
− chance of losing × net loss when losing This two-outcome formula applies to this all-in comparison. Ian's three kings cannot tie another hand on this board. Other spots may also need a tie term.
The charts start counting chips at the start of the river. Earlier contributions are already in the pot. Ian's 800,000 river bet still counts as a cost, so folding later shows −800,000, not zero.

Values are rounded for reading. The full API responses retain the numerical output. Decimal places do not establish accuracy.

4. First decision: how much should Ian bet?

At 02:35, before Ian bets, the pot is 1,410,000. He can check or choose one of the model's bet sizes.

Estimated net chips from the start of the river. Blue is an alternative. Gold is Ian's recorded move. Each row includes the model's possible later responses, not just the bet itself.

The 465,300 and 1,057,500 sizes are 33% and 75% of the starting pot. The model also includes Ian's recorded 800,000 bet and a bet that covers Joe's stack. It does not search every possible size.

The model ranks a 465,300 bet highest at about +2.053M chips. Ian's 800,000 bet is estimated at +2.038M.

An estimate can exceed the current pot because Joe can put in more chips before the hand ends.

The estimated gap is about 15,000 chips. That small gap is not proof that Ian chose the wrong size. The model does not certify the accuracy of this hand's estimates.

Joe does not always shove in these comparisons. The model includes his possible folds, calls, and raises. A 2,940,000 bet covers Joe's remaining stack; it does not use all of Ian's larger stack.

5. Second decision: call Joe's all-in or fold?

After Ian bets 800,000, Joe raises to 2,940,000 total. Ian must add 2,140,000 to match it.

FINAL POT IF HE CALLS= 7,290,000The pot is the total prize, not Ian's net profit.
Break-even win chance = 2,140,000 ÷ 7,290,000 = 29.4% From this decision onward, folding costs no more chips. With no ties, a call needs to win more than 29.4% of the time to beat folding on chip value alone.

The model estimates Ian wins about 88.3% of the time against the hands it has Joe raise all-in here. This is a result of the assumed ranges and strategy, not an observed fact about Joe.

6. The actual result was different from the model's average

BROADCAST CARDS, SHOWN HERE AS HINDSIGHT
Q♣10♦

Joe has queen-ten. With the shared J, K, and A, he has a straight: 10-J-Q-K-A. That beats Ian's three kings.

07:04, IAN FOLDS
−1,490,000 chips

That is Ian's actual loss across the whole hand: 690,000 before the river, plus his 800,000 river bet.

Against Joe's actual queen-ten, calling would have lost another 2,140,000 chips. Ian's fold saved those chips in this particular hand.

The useful conclusion is conditional. If Joe raises with the broad mix of hands in this model, calling ranks higher. If his real range is much stronger, that conclusion can reverse. The default-range result alone is not enough to judge Ian's fold.

Joe's broadcast cards are excluded from the solver inputs. Using them to justify a decision Ian made without seeing them would mix hindsight with decision analysis.

7. What this demonstration does and does not establish

The full flow works for this reviewed hand: recorded actions become a reconstructed game state, then assumed ranges, then estimated choices with video links.

The solver covers two-player rivers and a finite set of legal bet sizes. It ignores tournament payout effects, often called ICM. Those effects matter because tournament chips do not translate directly into prize money.

The measured solver gap is 0.48% of the starting pot, averaged across its ranges. That is not a 0.48% error bar on Ian's EV, and it does not validate the opponent range.

This example does not prove automatic understanding of an unseen video in under one second. There are no paid inference calls in this local analysis, but compute and video acquisition still have costs.
